What Is an Expert?

A working definition for Saarthi · March 2026
Internal Research

Five Properties of an Expert

Assembly

1. Calibrated Self-Knowledge

Knows the boundary of competence — precisely, not vaguely.

Junior: "I think this could be serious."
Expert: "I can't differentiate X from Y without creatinine. Order that first."

Two components: knowing what you have, and knowing what you lack. The second is harder and more valuable.

Softmax forces P(token) to sum to 1. No "I don't know" in output space. The model must always commit.
Assembly

2. Domain Compression

Doesn't process more data — processes the right data. 5,000 log lines → the 5 that matter.

Junior: checks every dashboard
Expert: eyes go straight to the one metric that matters

Information theory: experts have lower entropy within their domain. They know which distinctions matter.

Reasoning

3. Judgment Under Ambiguity

When evidence contradicts, reasons about why — doesn't average signals.

Novice: "Logs show no errors but metrics show 50% error rate. Conflicting data."
Expert: "The logging system is broken, not the service. Fix observability first."

Requires meta-reasoning: thinking about the structure of evidence, not just content.

Both

4. Principled Refusal

The defining property. An expert is defined by what they refuse to do.

Doctor: "I need an MRI before I can tell you. Acting without it risks misdiagnosis."
SRE: "I don't have enough to act safely. Let me gather more data."

Novices act to demonstrate competence. Experts refuse to demonstrate it.

Transformer architecture makes this structurally impossible. Softmax forces output. Training rewards fluency over abstention.
Assembly

5. Persistent Compounding Knowledge

Doesn't start from zero. Carries structured knowledge across interactions.

Oncologist on visit #5: "Last time we adjusted the dosing. Let me check if it worked."

Episodic + semantic + procedural memory. Current LLMs have none — every conversation starts fresh.

The Assembly-Reasoning Split

The critical finding from field deployment

Expertise has two components, often performed by different actors:

Intelligent assembly — knowing what context to gather, for whom, when. Not reasoning. Pattern matching on what-context-helps-when.

Domain reasoning — given assembled context, drawing conclusions and deciding actions.

In most high-stakes domains, the human is the reasoner. Doctors rejected AI reasoning. They want the right context at the right time.

3 of 5 expert properties live in assembly. 2 require reasoning. Assembly is where 60% of the expert value lives. Reasoning is an optional downstream consumer.

Updated definition

An expert is a system that assembles the right context for the right question, reasons over it with domain awareness when reasoning is needed, knows the limits of both its assembly and its reasoning, and improves both through experience.

State or Process?

Both, in sequence.

Configuration
Domain rules, entity graph, procedures
Calibration
Tune scoring, test outputs, adjust
Learning
Feedback loops, pattern memory
Expertise
Intuition from accumulated experience

Dreyfus model: Novice → Competent (state) → Proficient → Expert (process). Current LLMs are between 2-3. The gap is 4-5.

Implications

85%

Buildable now. Context engineering + constraints covers 85-90% of expert behavior on structured tasks. Commercially valuable today.

15%

Research frontier. Principled refusal, meta-reasoning, calibrated confidence. Requires architectural change. Disproportionately matters where it's needed most.

→

The bridge: Constraint layer — mandatory citations, contradiction detection, negation rules. Not guaranteed, but significantly safer than unconstrained generation.
